Executive Summary

Buy the trusted local provider. Centralize the operating system. Let AI do the rest.

AI-enabled roll-ups are emerging as a distinct company-creation model — neither vertical SaaS nor a private-equity buy-and-build with a thin technology veneer.

The strongest versions acquire or partner with legacy service businesses, take direct control of workflows, customers, data, and labor capacity, and then use AI plus centralized operations to expand throughput, improve margins, raise service quality, and create new revenue products.

The operating pattern is simple: buy the local trusted service provider, centralize the operating system, use AI to increase throughput, quality, and margin — then acquire more.

The most investable pattern is not “buy fragmented industry, add AI.” The better screen requires recurring or highly repeatable demand; measurable work queues; document-, message-, voice-, or ticket-heavy workflows; acute labor shortages; a fragmented owner base with succession pressure; and trust or regulation that makes direct ownership more effective than selling software from outside.

The highest-conviction verticals are accounting/tax/payroll, managed IT services, healthcare revenue-cycle and practice administration, and property management. Legal, construction/AEC, insurance services, and wealth/advisory are compelling but demand tighter regulatory and execution discipline. CX/BPO has the fastest automation path but the most intense competition from horizontal AI vendors.

The most investable AI roll-up is an operating company that acquires the right to redesign work.
Working Definition

What makes the model different

An AI-enabled roll-up controls legacy service businesses, then uses AI-native software, centralized operations, and shared data to transform the underlying delivery model.

General Catalyst’s “Transformations” strategy is the clearest public framing: backing founders who acquire, rebuild, and operate legacy service businesses with AI as the primary transformation lever. Its associated company set includes Accrual, Beacon Software, Crescendo, Dwelly, Eudia, Integral, Long Lake, Titan MSP, and Zero RFI. [1][2][3]

ModelPrimary assetHow value is createdMain limitation
Traditional vertical SaaSSoftware productSell tools into incumbent workflowsAdoption is slow when customers lack process maturity or incentive to change
Traditional PE roll-upAcquired EBITDACentralize finance, procurement, pricing, managementLimited organic transformation unless technology changes the work itself

The model works best where selling software alone is insufficient: distribution is hard, the buyer is too small or process-constrained to transform itself, and the customer buys an outcome rather than software. Direct ownership gives the platform the right to redesign the work.

Best-Fit Verticals by Thesis

Seven theses, in order of conviction

1 · Accounting, Tax & Payroll

The highest-density category — fragmented incumbents, recurring work, document-heavy processes, measurable productivity, compliance-driven demand, and a visible talent shortage. Wolters Kluwer counts more than 46,000 US accounting firms, reinforcing the acquisition supply. [47] Accrual, Crete, Multiplier, Integral, and Taxforce all attack variants of the same wedge. The platform must own the workflow, not merely sell software into it. The key underwriting question: can quality and liability controls scale faster than the volume of work?

2 · Managed IT Services / MSP

The cleanest recurring-revenue analogue. Titan MSP and Shield show the same pattern — consolidate, centralize AI engineering, apply agents to tickets, alerts, troubleshooting, and remediation. The attraction is measurability: time-to-resolution, backlog, first-contact resolution, utilization, margin, churn. The risk is touching customers’ operational infrastructure; the winning model compresses triage first, then expands into constrained remediation.

3 · Healthcare Revenue Cycle & Practice Admin

One of the largest pools of automatable white-collar work. The best targets are not clinical providers but RCM firms, billing companies, coding shops, prior-auth vendors, and patient-access call centers. Commure reportedly completes >85% of RCM work without human intervention across 500+ organizations and 3,000 sites. [43][44][45][46] Slower and more politically sensitive than accounting or MSP — the best version is specialty-specific RCM with measurable denials, collections, and recovered revenue.

4 · Property Management & Lettings

The most visibly European wedge. Dwelly, Buena, Arbio, and hallo theo point to the same opportunity: local, under-digitized providers with communications-heavy workflows and owners open to succession liquidity. The challenge is operational messiness — repairs, tenants, landlords, local rules, physical assets — which is exactly why ownership matters.

5 · Legal Services

High-value workflows, harder to scale operationally. Eudia’s Johnson Hana acquisition adds 300+ legal professionals — relationships, process knowledge, and human-in-the-loop review. The plausible wedge is not replacing lawyers broadly but transforming repeatable legal operations, compliance review, intake, and contract analysis under accuracy, privilege, and licensing constraints.

6 · CX / BPO

Fastest automation path, weakest moat unless the platform owns outcomes. Crescendo’s PartnerHero acquisition adds delivery capacity and an outcome-priced hybrid model. Durable advantage depends on vertical specialization, workflow ownership, proprietary service data, and pricing around resolved outcomes rather than seats.

7 · Construction / AEC Services

The emerging frontier. Zero RFI moves the model beyond back-office work into capital projects — owner-rep expertise, lifecycle documentation, industry network. RFIs, submittals, closeout packages, and owner reporting are natural AI surfaces; the difficulty is that every project is different, liability is high, and field adoption is slow.

The Europe Opportunity

Underpriced, and country-by-country

Europe’s fragmented services sectors hold more than $4tn across information, professional, and administrative services — many workflows still text- and voice-heavy. [2]

The opportunity is less “replace an incumbent SaaS tool” and more “bring software and AI into service sectors that never fully adopted vertical software.” The clearest wedges: property management and lettings (Dwelly, Buena, Arbio, hallo theo); tax, accounting and payroll (Integral, Taxforce); legal delivery; SME administrative services; and hospitality/rental operations.

Tenet’s Berlin-based AI roll-up fund is another signal, explicitly targeting European professional-services roll-ups and arguing that lower vertical-SaaS penetration creates an opening for AI-native operators to buy service businesses rather than sell tools to them. [10]

Three features favor the model: high fragmentation at the local provider level; compliance and language complexity that creates defensible operational know-how; and lower historical vertical-SaaS penetration, which makes software-enabled ownership more powerful than incremental procurement.

The catch is that cross-border scaling is harder than in the US. Labor law, tax rules, licensing, language, and consumer regulation differ materially by country. The winning European platforms may look more like country-by-country operating systems than one universal pan-European platform.

Strategic Extension

From single vertical to holding company

In the stronger version, a permanent-capital holding company owns multiple operating businesses and applies one shared AI operating system across them.

DimensionSingle-vertical roll-upAI-native holding company
ScopeOne categoryMultiple categories, shared primitives
CapitalBuy-and-build, vertical financingPermanent / long-horizon, compounds across industries
AI assetWorkflow automation in one verticalShared OS, model routing, data pipelines, deployment playbooks
Data edgeProprietary vertical dataCross-company loops + domain fine-tuning per business
Main riskIntegration & executionStrategic overreach; proving one OS generalizes

Beacon Software and Long Lake show the strategy widening beyond one vertical — Beacon as the broadest horizontal platform [23][24], Long Lake’s $6.3bn Amex GBT deal as the largest visible services-transformation bet. [25][26]

Ten questions before underwriting

QuestionWhat good looks like

Bottom Line

Conviction, ranked

Near-term highest conviction. Accounting/tax/payroll (strongest density of proof points); MSP/IT services (cleanest recurring-revenue loop); healthcare RCM/admin (largest admin-cost pool, heavier regulation); property management/lettings (strongest Europe-first category).

Holding-company archetypes to watch. Thrive Holdings (services), Project Prometheus (industrial), Special (critical Main Street services).

High-upside but harder. Legal services, construction/AEC, insurance services, wealth/advisory, home and field services.

Crowded but automatable. CX/BPO. Useful precedent, less AI-native: auto repair and local roll-ups; vertical software holdcos such as Beacon.

The practical underwriting question: does this holding company have a repeatable AI deployment advantage — or is it merely a capital allocator buying old-economy businesses at a higher multiple because AI is in the story?

The winner may not be the best consolidator in one vertical, but the best AI deployment operator across many.
